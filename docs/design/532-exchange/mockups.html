The exchange, four ways

You asked for three genuinely different designs per piece, not three settings of one dial. So each set below starts from a different premise. Mix across sets freely — they share the same cards, the same seats and the same rules, so 1B + 2C + 3A + 4B is a real answer, not a compromise.

One thing the research settled that shapes all of it: Murlan's "you may only give a 3 to a 10" is this game's own rule. Every other game in the family lets the winner give any card. So the screen is not just pointing at which cards are legal — it is teaching a rule the player has never met.

Set 1

Asking for the card

The moment before the pick. The centre of the table is free — nothing is flying yet — so this is the one instant a prompt can own it. All three vacate the centre the moment you choose, because the flight needs that space back.

A

The nameplate

A plate at the centre: their avatar, their name, one instruction. Reads at a glance and cannot be missed.

Against it: it is a floating panel — the most conventional answer, and the one that feels most like a dialog.

B

The seat speaks

No centre panel at all. Their seat lifts, brightens, and says it in a tag pointing at them. The request comes from the person.

Against it: a corner seat is further from where your eyes are, and on a small phone the tag is tight.

C

Their card asks

The card they gave you lands face-up in the middle and the words sit on the felt under it. No panel, no chrome — the object is the prompt.

Against it: quietest of the three. Someone in a hurry may tap a card before reading why.

What to weigh: A is loudest and safest. C is the most in-world and reuses a card you have to draw anyway. B is the only one that makes the recipient a person rather than a name in a sentence — which is what you asked for when you said "maybe show his avatar too".

Set 2

Which cards you may give

Your whole hand is visible now — that was the complaint. Ranks 3 to 10 are legal; everything else is not. Accessibility rules out colour as the only channel, so each option also changes something a monochrome screenshot would show.

A

Glow and dim

Legal cards get a gold rim and lift. Illegal ones drop to a third opacity and lose their colour. The pairing is what Hearthstone does, and both halves matter.

Against it: says which, never why. A first-time player learns the rule by elimination.

B

Two rows

Legal cards step forward into their own row; the rest sit back and lower. Distinction by position — survives colour-blindness, dark rooms and a cracked screen.

Against it: breaks the fan you have spent tickets getting right, and takes vertical space a phone in landscape does not have.

C

The rule, drawn

A gold band laid across the hand over ranks 3–10, labelled. It does not point at cards — it draws the rule itself, once, where the cards are.

Against it: only works while the hand stays sorted by rank. Reordering (#531) would break it.

What to weigh: A is the known-good default. C is the only one that answers the question the research says actually matters — it teaches the rule instead of enforcing it silently — but it ties this screen to a sorted hand, and you have a separate ticket about letting players reorder. A and C can be combined: band on first exposure, glow-and-dim after.

Set 3

Both cards moving at once

Simultaneous, opposite, never overlapping. There is a perceptual reason and not just a practical one: motion is the strongest grouping cue there is, so two cards travelling together read as one thing. Opposite direction is what makes them two gifts.

A

Two lanes

Straight across, opposite directions, at different heights. Already how ExchangeAnnouncement works today — so this is the cheapest and the most proven.

Against it: nothing about it says the two cards have anything to do with each other.

B

Over and under

Both arc through the centre, one passing over the other. They cross in depth rather than in space, so they share one lane and still never collide.

Against it: depth is faked with scale and shadow. On a small screen the pass can read as a near-miss.

C

Meet and part

Both travel to the centre, touch for one beat, then continue to the far side. The contact is what makes it read as a trade rather than two unrelated gifts.

Against it: longest of the three, and the pause is a beat the player did not ask for.

What to weigh: A is free — the code exists. C is the only one that says exchange rather than two deliveries, and the beat in the middle is where the satisfying part lives. B is the compromise and is the weakest on a phone.

Set 4

Saying what just happened

You asked for text that does not cover the action. Conventional guidance says keep the middle of the screen clear during play — which is right, and does not contradict Set 1, because that prompt is gone by the time these run.

A

A line on the felt

One low-contrast line printed on the felt, below the flight path. Reads as part of the table rather than something laid over it.

Against it: low contrast on felt is exactly the readability trap. Needs a real contrast check, not a taste call.

B

Arrows, no words

Two chevrons, one trailing each card, each in its owner's colour. Nothing to read, nothing to translate, no line length to fit in three languages.

Against it: tells you a swap happened, never who gained. And it adds motion to a frame that is already the busiest one.

C

Tags at the seats

A small tag beside each seat, lighting as that seat's card lands. The words sit where the people are — furthest from the action, and it names what each side got.

Against it: two things to read in two places, at the moment your eyes are following a card.

What to weigh: B is the safest and the only one that costs nothing to translate. C is the only one that survives being watched from a seat that is not in the exchange — the other two players are also looking at this screen, and they care who got what.

Settled before the mockups

  • The GIOCA button is the confirm. You asked for the normal table with the exchange layered on. A second floating confirm button is the old modal in a smaller coat, so none of these has one.
  • The loser never chooses. Their strongest card is taken automatically the moment the phase opens — only the winner picks. That is the rule today and none of this changes it.
  • The two-way flight already exists in ExchangeAnnouncement, wired to the wrong moment. Set 3A is that code moved, not new code.

How to answer

One letter per set — for example "1B, 2A+C, 3C, 4B". Mixing is expected; the sets were built to be independent.
